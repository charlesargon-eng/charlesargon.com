Research

I work on China since 1500 and on empire as a comparative problem. One question runs through both: how ideas about governing survived the journey from the capital to the county, and what they looked like on arrival. Three areas make up most of my current work; articles drawing on them are in preparation.

Baojia and the boundaries of political community

My first book, developed from my dissertation, takes up baojia, the Qing framework for registering households and organizing neighborhoods for mutual responsibility and local security. Standard accounts treat it as an institution in slow decline. I read it instead as a contest, in which emperors, officials, local elites, and ordinary households pressed on the question of who belonged to a political community and on what terms. A case study of Jiangxi anchors the argument. The work draws on state archives and documents, legal cases, local gazetteers, and diverse literary sources and private writings.

The lives of ideas in the Qing state

A second area follows the knowledge officials actually used. Magistrate handbooks, collected works, and statecraft writing carried a practical literature on how to hold office: how to manage subordinates, read a register, run an inspection, govern a place one had never seen. I want to know how that literature was organized, how it changed over the dynasty, and how far it shaped what officials did. My article on Dai Zhaochen, a prefect in Shandong and Guangdong in the 1860s, is the published entry point: an intellectual biography of one bureaucrat, and an account of what statecraft ideas looked like once they had to be executed.

Quantitative History & Data

My research on Qing governance raises broader questions about how historians use data. Much of the surviving record of Qing local governance takes the form of long, repetitive documentary series: contracts, registers, regulations, bibliographies. Any one document is thin. In aggregate they answer questions about distribution, variation, and change that close reading of a few cases cannot. Building datasets from such series raises its own problems: how to transcribe and code the data, the biases of survival and publication, and how to report statistical findings. I am a contributing author on HistBench and HistAgent, a benchmark and agent for multimodal historical reasoning, and in 2024–2025 I co-organized an AI and History Working Group at Princeton.
